# Copy on screen

Captions are prose a person reads, so the `human-prose` skill applies to every one.
Nothing is copied from the product's store metadata or README without that check: store
copy is often AI-written too.

## The voiceover script

Send the user the full script before any voice is generated. When the user rewrites it, their text replaces the draft word for word, in every language they rewrote.

- Open on one moment from the viewer's day, in full sentences, and let the sentence run across the first cut into what goes wrong: "You're watching your favourite show episode nineteen. And as you're about to press play, / you're somewhere else."
- Write what a person says aloud to a friend: whole clauses, contractions, and the small words speech uses ("so", "even", "actually", "btw"). Fragments and lists fail: "Download. Close. Next."
- Each line names who does what to the viewer, with a plain verb for the harm: "The site draws an invisible sheet covering the entire page, which steals your clicks." A pronoun with nothing to point at fails: "Here is how a page takes it."
- Say what the viewer suffers and gets. Cut every beat that explains how the product decides: a rule, a threshold, a block list, a timing number. A buyer needs the threat and the rescue.
- Merge several mechanisms into one line when the film only needs their effect.
- End by returning to the opening moment, with its problem gone.
- Mark delivery in the script: `**word**` for stress, `[pause]`, `[suspense]`. The voice take and the cut both follow the marks.

## Each language has its own script

Compose each language from the meaning, with the words its speakers say aloud, English loanwords included. Indonesian takes "link", "pencet", "dicek", "nyuri", "gajelas", and fails on the dictionary forms "tautan", "menekannya", "memeriksa", "mencuri". Use the language's interjections and particles ("Lah,", "deh"). A language may merge or drop a beat the other keeps: compare meaning per film, never clause per clause.

## Captions

A caption is a complete thought with its actor, at most 8 words, read alone and muted by a buyer who has never seen the product. Cut it from the script line under it, keeping the verb and the object. A noun-phrase label only the voiceover explains fails and gets rewritten:

| Fails | Reads alone |
|---|---|
| Swapped mid-press | It changes the link after you click |
| An invisible layer | An invisible sheet steals your clicks |
| Pulled right back | Undirect forces you straight back |
| Ditukar saat ditekan | Link-nya diubah setelah dipencet |

## What trusted human copy looks like

`<library>/copy/README.md` holds the text read (OCR) from about
490 reference frames, tiered by how far each source can be trusted as human-written.

| Measured on the trusted tier | Value |
|---|---|
| Shape | verb + concrete object: "Accept any payment type", "Sync clipboard history", "Sell in-store and online" |
| Lists | literal, one item per line, as many as there really are (Wipr lists fifteen blocked things) |
| Where the words are | mostly in the product UI itself; the overlay headline is short or absent |

Tier D sources (Dribbble shots, YouTube tutorial demos) hold the tells: "designed to
revolutionize the way", "streamline your operations and empower you". Use them for
visuals only.

## Rules

- One caption per beat. A subline is optional and says a fact the screen
  cannot show.
- Every claim is true of the product today and every number comes from a source you can
  name (README, code, store listing). Write down the approved numbers first (product-launch-motion law 1).
- The caption says what the screen proves. If the screen already says it, cut the caption.
- No antithesis, no "No X. No Y.", no triads for rhythm, none of the inflated words in
  `human-prose` Part 1.
- Non-English cuts are written in the language from the screen, never translated from the
  English caption (`blind-translation`).
- Hold each caption long enough to read: at most 20 characters per second, and no less
  than 5/6 s on screen (Netflix timed-text guide).
